# What is nuclear fission?

## Short Answer

Nuclear fission is the process where a heavy atomic nucleus splits into two or more lighter nuclei, releasing energy and often neutrons. Fission occurs when a nucleus absorbs a neutron and becomes unstable, splitting apart. Fission powers nuclear reactors and atomic bombs.

## Detailed Explanation

### Background

Nuclear fission is the process that powers nuclear reactors and was used in atomic bombs, representing humanity's ability to harness nuclear energy. Understanding nuclear fission helps us comprehend how nuclear power works, how atomic weapons function, and how we can control nuclear reactions. This knowledge is essential for understanding nuclear physics and nuclear technology.

Fission demonstrates how splitting heavy nuclei releases energy through mass-energy conversion (E=mc²), with fission products having less total mass than the original nucleus. This mass difference converts to enormous energy. By exploring nuclear fission, we can better understand nuclear energy and its applications.

Understanding nuclear fission connects to many fundamental physics concepts. The principles relate to concepts like [What is nuclear fusion?](what-is-nuclear-fusion), which is the opposite process, and [What is E=mc²?](what-is-equals-mc-squared), which describes the energy release.

Nuclear fission was discovered in 1938 by Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann, with Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch providing the theoretical explanation. This discovery led directly to the development of nuclear reactors and atomic weapons. Today, nuclear fission provides about 10% of the world's electricity, demonstrating how understanding fundamental physics can lead to transformative technologies.

### Scientific Principles

Nuclear fission works through several key principles:

1. **Neutron absorption**: Fission typically begins when a heavy nucleus (like uranium-235) absorbs a neutron, becoming unstable and splitting into two lighter nuclei (fission products).

2. **Chain reaction**: Fission releases neutrons that can cause more fissions, creating a chain reaction. Controlled chain reactions power nuclear reactors, while uncontrolled reactions create atomic explosions.

3. **Mass-energy conversion**: Fission products have less total mass than the original nucleus. This mass difference converts to energy via E=mc², releasing enormous energy—fission releases millions of times more energy per mass than chemical reactions.

4. **Fission products**: Fission produces two lighter nuclei (often radioactive), neutrons, and energy. Common fission products include barium, krypton, and other elements, often radioactive.

5. **Critical mass**: A chain reaction requires sufficient fissile material (critical mass). Too little material, and neutrons escape without causing fissions. Too much, and the reaction becomes uncontrolled.

6. **Moderation**: Nuclear reactors use moderators (like water or graphite) to slow down neutrons, making them more likely to cause fission in uranium-235. Fast neutrons are less effective at causing fission.

7. **Control rods**: Control rods made of materials that absorb neutrons (like boron or cadmium) are used to control reactor power. Inserting rods reduces neutron flux and slows the reaction; removing them increases power.

### Real Examples

- Nuclear reactors: nuclear power plants use controlled fission of uranium-235 or plutonium-239, with control rods moderating the reaction to maintain steady power output.

- Atomic bombs: atomic weapons use uncontrolled fission chain reactions, with critical mass assembled rapidly to create an explosive chain reaction releasing enormous energy.

- Nuclear waste: fission produces radioactive waste products that must be safely stored, with understanding fission essential for managing nuclear waste and safety.

- Radioisotope production: fission reactors produce radioisotopes for medical and industrial use, with fission creating isotopes used in medicine, research, and industry.

- Natural fission: some natural deposits can undergo spontaneous fission, with understanding fission explaining natural nuclear reactors that existed billions of years ago.

## Practical Applications

### How It Works in Daily Life

Understanding nuclear fission helps us in many ways:

1. **Nuclear power**: Nuclear reactors provide electricity through controlled fission, supplying significant portions of electricity in many countries and providing low-carbon energy.

2. **Medical applications**: Fission-produced radioisotopes are used in medical imaging and treatment, with understanding fission essential for nuclear medicine.

3. **Industrial applications**: Radioisotopes from fission are used in industry for measurement, sterilization, and other applications, demonstrating practical uses of fission products.

4. **Scientific research**: Understanding fission helps research nuclear physics, materials science, and other fields, advancing scientific knowledge.

5. **Safety and waste management**: Understanding fission is essential for nuclear safety and managing radioactive waste, ensuring safe operation of nuclear facilities.
