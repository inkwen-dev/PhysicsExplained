# What is absolute zero?

## Short Answer

Absolute zero is the theoretical lowest possible temperature, at which particles have minimal motion and no thermal energy can be removed. It's defined as 0 Kelvin (-273.15°C or -459.67°F) and represents the point where entropy reaches its minimum value.

## Detailed Explanation

### Background

Absolute zero represents one of the most fundamental limits in physics—the coldest temperature possible. Understanding absolute zero helps us grasp the nature of temperature, energy, and the behavior of matter at extreme conditions. This concept is central to thermodynamics and has profound implications for our understanding of the universe.

The concept of absolute zero emerged from studies of how gases behave as they cool. Scientists observed that as temperature decreases, gas volume decreases proportionally, suggesting that at some point, volume would reach zero. This theoretical limit became known as absolute zero, and it's fundamental to understanding temperature scales and thermal behavior.

Understanding absolute zero connects to many fundamental physics principles. The principles behind absolute zero relate to concepts like [What is temperature?](what-is-temperature), which describes how temperature measures particle motion, and [What is entropy?](what-is-entropy), which reaches its minimum at absolute zero according to the third law of thermodynamics.

### Scientific Principles

Absolute zero works through several key principles:

1. **Zero particle motion**: At absolute zero, particles would have minimal possible motion (quantum mechanics shows they can't be completely still due to zero-point energy). This represents the state of minimum thermal energy.

2. **Kelvin temperature scale**: Absolute zero defines the Kelvin temperature scale, where 0 K is absolute zero. Kelvin is an absolute scale, meaning it starts from a true zero point, unlike Celsius and Fahrenheit which have arbitrary zero points.

3. **Third law of thermodynamics**: The third law states that as temperature approaches absolute zero, entropy approaches a constant minimum value. This law explains why absolute zero cannot be reached in a finite number of steps.

4. **Unattainability**: While scientists have achieved temperatures extremely close to absolute zero (within billionths of a degree), absolute zero itself cannot be reached because it would require infinite time or infinite steps to remove all thermal energy.

5. **Quantum mechanical limit**: Even at absolute zero, quantum mechanics predicts that particles retain some energy (zero-point energy) due to the uncertainty principle, meaning complete stillness is impossible.

### Real Examples

- Laboratory achievements: Scientists have cooled atoms to temperatures within billionths of a degree above absolute zero using laser cooling and evaporative cooling techniques, creating Bose-Einstein condensates.

- Space temperatures: The cosmic microwave background radiation, the afterglow of the Big Bang, has a temperature of about 2.7 K, very close to absolute zero but not quite there.

- Superconductivity: Some materials become superconductors (zero electrical resistance) at very low temperatures, though not at absolute zero. This demonstrates how low temperatures enable unique material properties.

- Liquid helium: Helium remains liquid at temperatures very close to absolute zero (below 4.2 K at atmospheric pressure), demonstrating unusual quantum behavior at extremely low temperatures.

- Quantum effects: At temperatures near absolute zero, quantum mechanical effects become dominant, leading to phenomena like superfluidity and Bose-Einstein condensation that don't occur at higher temperatures.

## Practical Applications

### How It Works in Daily Life

Understanding absolute zero helps us in many ways:

1. **Temperature measurement**: The Kelvin scale, based on absolute zero, is used in scientific research and provides an absolute reference for temperature measurements, essential for precise scientific work.

2. **Cryogenics and cooling**: Understanding absolute zero helps design cooling systems for applications like MRI machines, particle accelerators, and space telescopes, which require extremely low temperatures.

3. **Material science**: Scientists study materials near absolute zero to discover new properties, such as superconductivity and superfluidity, which have applications in technology and research.

4. **Quantum computing**: Some quantum computers operate at temperatures near absolute zero to minimize thermal noise and maintain quantum coherence, enabling more stable quantum operations.

5. **Fundamental research**: Research near absolute zero helps scientists study fundamental physics, test quantum mechanics, and understand the behavior of matter at its most extreme conditions.

- Research cryogenics: learn about how scientists achieve extremely low temperatures using techniques like laser cooling, evaporative cooling, and dilution refrigeration to approach absolute zero.

- Explore quantum effects: study how materials behave differently near absolute zero, with quantum mechanical effects becoming dominant and enabling phenomena like superconductivity and superfluidity.

- Calculate conversions: practice converting between temperature scales, understanding that absolute zero is -273.15°C, -459.67°F, and 0 K, demonstrating the relationships between scales.
