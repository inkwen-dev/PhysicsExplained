# What is Coulomb's law?

## Short Answer

Coulomb's law describes the force between two electric charges. It states that the force is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. Like charges repel (positive force) and opposite charges attract (negative force).

## Detailed Explanation

### Background

Coulomb's law is one of the most fundamental laws in physics, describing how electric charges interact with each other. Named after French physicist Charles-Augustin de Coulomb, this law is the electrical equivalent of Newton's law of universal gravitation and forms the foundation of electrostatics. Understanding Coulomb's law helps us predict and explain electric forces in everything from atomic structure to electrical circuits.

This law is essential because it precisely describes the force between charges, allowing us to calculate electric forces and understand how charges interact. The law's inverse-square relationship (force decreases with distance squared) appears throughout physics and helps explain many electrical phenomena. By exploring Coulomb's law, we can better understand electric fields, electrical systems, and fundamental forces.

Understanding Coulomb's law connects to many fundamental physics concepts. The principles behind Coulomb's law relate to concepts like [Why do like charges repel?](why-do-like-charges-repel), which describes charge interactions, and [What is an electric field?](what-is-an-electric-field), which is derived from Coulomb's law.

### Scientific Principles

Coulomb's law works through several key principles:

1. **Force formula**: The force between two charges is F = k × (q₁ × q₂)/r², where k is Coulomb's constant (8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²), q₁ and q₂ are the charges, and r is the distance between them.

2. **Proportional to charge product**: Force is directly proportional to the product of the charges. Doubling either charge doubles the force; doubling both charges quadruples the force.

3. **Inverse square law**: Force decreases with the square of distance. Doubling the distance reduces force to 1/4; tripling distance reduces force to 1/9, showing the inverse-square relationship.

4. **Direction**: Like charges (both positive or both negative) produce positive force (repulsion), while opposite charges produce negative force (attraction). The force acts along the line connecting the charges.

5. **Vector nature**: Force is a vector quantity with both magnitude and direction. For multiple charges, forces add vectorially, requiring vector addition to find net force.

6. **Superposition principle**: When multiple charges are present, the total force on any charge is the vector sum of forces from all other charges. Each pair of charges interacts independently according to Coulomb's law.

### Real Examples

- Atomic structure: electrons are held in atoms by Coulomb attraction to the positive nucleus. The force follows Coulomb's law, with the inverse-square relationship determining electron orbits.

- Static electricity: when you rub objects together, charges transfer. The repulsion or attraction between charged objects follows Coulomb's law, with force decreasing as objects move apart.

- Lightning: lightning occurs when charge separation creates large potential differences. The electric forces involved follow Coulomb's law, with charges attracting or repelling based on the law.

- Particle accelerators: particle accelerators use electric fields based on Coulomb's law to accelerate charged particles, with forces calculated using Coulomb's law principles.

- Chemical bonding: ionic bonds form when opposite charges attract according to Coulomb's law, with the force holding ions together in crystal structures.

## Practical Applications

### How It Works in Daily Life

Understanding Coulomb's law helps us in many ways:

1. **Electrical engineering**: Engineers use Coulomb's law to design electrical systems, calculate forces in circuits, and understand charge interactions in electronic devices.

2. **Atomic and molecular physics**: Understanding Coulomb's law is essential for atomic structure, explaining how electrons are held in atoms and how molecules form through charge interactions.

3. **Particle physics**: Particle accelerators and detectors use Coulomb's law principles to manipulate and detect charged particles, with forces calculated using Coulomb's law.

4. **Material science**: Understanding charge interactions through Coulomb's law helps explain material properties, chemical bonding, and how materials respond to electric fields.

5. **Safety and design**: Understanding electric forces helps assess electrical safety, design safe systems, and predict how charges will behave in various situations.

### Scientific Experiments & Demonstrations

You can demonstrate Coulomb's law with simple experiments:

- Measure force vs distance: use charged objects and measure how force changes with distance, observing the inverse-square relationship as force decreases with distance squared.

- Measure force vs charge: vary the charges on objects and measure forces, observing how force is proportional to the product of charges, demonstrating Coulomb's law's charge dependence.

- Use an electroscope: charge an electroscope and observe how forces between charges affect the leaves, demonstrating Coulomb's law in action.

- Calculate forces: use Coulomb's law formula to calculate forces between charges at different distances, comparing calculations with observations and understanding the mathematical relationship.

- Study field visualization: observe or create visualizations of electric fields around charges, understanding how Coulomb's law determines field strength and direction.

- Calculate multiple charges: use Coulomb's law to calculate forces in systems with multiple charges, using vector addition to find net forces and understanding how superposition works.
