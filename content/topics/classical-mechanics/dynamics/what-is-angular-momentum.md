# What is angular momentum?

## Short Answer

Angular momentum is a measure of how much rotational motion an object has, calculated as the product of its moment of inertia (rotational mass) and angular velocity (rotational speed). Like linear momentum, angular momentum is conserved unless acted upon by an external torque.

## Detailed Explanation

### Background

Angular momentum is the rotational equivalent of linear momentum, describing how objects spin and rotate. Just as linear momentum describes how much "oomph" an object has when moving in a straight line, angular momentum describes how much rotational motion an object possesses. This concept is fundamental to understanding everything from spinning tops to figure skaters to planets orbiting stars.

Understanding angular momentum is essential because rotational motion is everywhere in our universe. From the spin of a basketball to the rotation of Earth to the orbits of planets, angular momentum helps us understand and predict rotational behavior. The conservation of angular momentum—the principle that angular momentum remains constant unless external forces act—explains many fascinating phenomena we observe daily.

The concept of angular momentum connects to many practical applications and fundamental physics principles. Understanding angular momentum relates to concepts like [What is momentum?](what-is-momentum), which describes linear motion, and [What is torque?](what-is-torque), which is the rotational equivalent of force and can change angular momentum.

Angular momentum conservation is one of the most powerful principles in physics, explaining phenomena from the microscopic scale (electron spin) to the cosmic scale (galaxy rotation). The fact that angular momentum is conserved unless external torques act makes it a fundamental tool for understanding and predicting rotational motion throughout the universe.

### Scientific Principles

Angular momentum works through several key principles:

1. **Rotational equivalent of momentum**: Angular momentum (L) is calculated as L = I × ω, where I is moment of inertia (rotational mass) and ω is angular velocity (rotational speed). This parallels linear momentum (p = m × v).

2. **Conservation principle**: Angular momentum is conserved in a closed system—it remains constant unless an external torque acts on the system. This conservation explains many rotational phenomena.

3. **Vector quantity**: Angular momentum is a vector quantity with both magnitude and direction. The direction is perpendicular to the plane of rotation, following the right-hand rule.

4. **Moment of inertia**: The moment of inertia depends on how mass is distributed relative to the axis of rotation. Objects with mass farther from the axis have larger moments of inertia and thus more angular momentum for the same angular velocity.

5. **Angular velocity relationship**: For a given angular momentum, if moment of inertia decreases (mass moves closer to axis), angular velocity increases to maintain conservation. This explains why spinning objects speed up when they contract.

6. **Precession**: When a torque is applied perpendicular to angular momentum, it causes precession—the axis of rotation itself rotates. This explains phenomena like the wobbling of spinning tops and the precession of Earth's axis.

### Real Examples

- A figure skater spinning: when a skater pulls their arms in, their moment of inertia decreases, so their angular velocity increases to conserve angular momentum, causing them to spin faster.

- A spinning top: a top maintains its angular momentum, resisting changes in its orientation. The conservation of angular momentum keeps it spinning upright until friction gradually reduces it.

- Earth's rotation: Earth has angular momentum from its daily rotation. This angular momentum remains relatively constant over time, contributing to the stability of Earth's rotation axis.

- A bicycle wheel: when spinning, a bicycle wheel has angular momentum. If you try to tilt it, you feel resistance because changing its orientation requires changing its angular momentum.

- A planet orbiting the sun: planets have angular momentum from their orbital motion. This angular momentum is conserved, which is why planets maintain stable orbits.

## Practical Applications

### How It Works in Daily Life

Understanding angular momentum helps us in many ways:

1. **Sports and athletics**: Athletes use angular momentum principles in sports like figure skating, diving, and gymnastics, controlling their rotation by changing their moment of inertia (pulling in or extending limbs).

2. **Gyroscopes and navigation**: Gyroscopes use conservation of angular momentum to maintain orientation, providing stable reference frames for navigation systems in aircraft, ships, and spacecraft.

3. **Satellite stabilization**: Spacecraft use angular momentum and reaction wheels to control their orientation in space, using conservation principles to maintain stable attitudes without fuel.

4. **Engineering and machinery**: Engineers design rotating machinery understanding angular momentum, ensuring stability and efficiency in systems like turbines, engines, and flywheels.

5. **Astronomy and planetary science**: Scientists use angular momentum conservation to understand planetary formation, stellar rotation, and the dynamics of galaxies and star systems.

### Scientific Experiments & Demonstrations

You can demonstrate angular momentum with simple experiments:

- Spin on a rotating chair: sit on a rotating chair with weights in your hands. Extend your arms and spin, then pull your arms in. You'll spin faster when your arms are pulled in because your moment of inertia decreases, conserving angular momentum.

- Spin a bicycle wheel: hold a spinning bicycle wheel by its axle and try to tilt it. You'll feel resistance and the wheel will try to maintain its orientation, demonstrating angular momentum conservation.

- Use a spinning top: observe how a spinning top maintains its orientation and resists falling over due to conservation of angular momentum, until friction gradually reduces it.

- Compare spinning objects: spin objects of different shapes (like a disk vs a hoop) with the same mass and angular velocity. The hoop has more angular momentum because its mass is distributed farther from the axis.

- Demonstrate with a rotating platform: stand on a rotating platform and extend or contract your body. Observe how your rotation speed changes as you change your moment of inertia while conserving angular momentum.

- Calculate angular momentum: measure the mass, radius, and angular velocity of rotating objects, then calculate their angular momentum using L = Iω, understanding how different factors contribute to rotational motion.
